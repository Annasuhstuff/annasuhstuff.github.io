05 Reading 1 · Who invented chess? · ~8 min

The birth of a game 🇮🇳

Read the text once quickly. Don't stop at every unknown word — guess from context. The words in bold are explained under the text.

Four armies on one board

1Nobody knows the name of the person who invented chess, and it is quite possible that nobody ever will. What historians do agree on is where and roughly when it happened: in northern India, some time before the year 600. The game was called chaturanga, a Sanskrit word meaning "four divisions" — the four parts of an ancient Indian army: the infantry, the cavalry, the elephants and the chariots. Look at a modern chessboard and you can still see them: the pawns are the foot soldiers, the knights are the horsemen, the bishops were once elephants, and the rooks were war chariots.

2A famous legend tells a different story. A wise man, it is said, invented the game for a bored king. The king was so delighted that he offered the inventor any reward he wanted. The wise man asked for something that sounded modest: one grain of rice on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, and so on, doubling each time. The king laughed and agreed — and then his mathematicians discovered that there was not enough rice in the whole world to pay him. The story is almost certainly not true, but it is still the best way ever invented to explain how fast numbers can grow.

3From India the game travelled west to Persia, where it became shatranj. The Persians introduced two words that we still shout today. When a player attacked the king, he said "Shah!" — "King!". When the king could not escape, he said "Shah mat!" — "the king is helpless". Say those two words quickly in English and you get check and checkmate.

4In the 7th century the Arabs conquered Persia, fell in love with shatranj, and carried it across their empire. By the 10th century it had reached Spain and Italy; a hundred years later it was being played all over Europe, from Sicily to Iceland. The Church was not happy: several bishops tried to ban the game because players gambled on it, and one 12th-century priest, who was forbidden to play, is said to have invented the folding chessboard so that his board would look like two harmless books on a shelf.

08 Reading 2 · How did they play? · ~8 min

The game that kept changing ⏳

The chess of 1200 was not the chess we play now. Read and find out what changed — and who changed it.

The slow game and the mad queen

1If you sat down opposite a medieval player, you would recognise the board, but you would probably lose your patience long before you lost the game. Old chess was painfully slow. The queen — who was then a male adviser called a vizier — could move only one square diagonally. The bishop, still an elephant, jumped exactly two squares and could reach only eight squares on the whole board. Pawns moved one step at a time; there was no castling. Games could last for days, and players often started from fixed positions to skip the boring beginning.

2Then, quite suddenly, everything changed. Around 1475, somewhere in Spain or Italy, the vizier turned into a queen and was given the power to move as far as she liked in any direction. The bishop was set free at the same time. Contemporary players were shocked by the speed of the new game and gave it a nickname: scacchi alla rabiosa — "mad queen chess". Nobody knows exactly who suggested the change, but historians point out that Europe was then ruled by several very powerful queens, and Isabella of Castile was on the Spanish throne. Whatever the reason, the new rules spread across Europe in about fifty years, and the game we play today was essentially born.

3Two problems remained. The first was that there was no agreement about the details: castling, en passant and stalemate worked differently in different countries, and travelling players had to ask about the local rules before they sat down. The second was time. In the 19th century a player could think about a single move for two hours; in one famous match a game had to be stopped because both players had fallen asleep. In 1861 sand-glasses were used in a match in England, and in 1883 the first mechanical chess clock appeared. Suddenly thinking too long could cost you the game.

4The modern era began with two events. The first international tournament was held in London in 1851 and was won by a German teacher, Adolf Anderssen. Thirty-five years later, in 1886, Wilhelm Steinitz beat Johannes Zukertort in a match and became the first official World Champion — a title that has never been vacant since. Steinitz also changed the way people thought about the game: instead of wild attacks, he taught that you should build up small advantages patiently. Many players found this deeply boring. He was right anyway.

11 Reading 3 · Fun facts · ~7 min

Numbers that don't fit in your head 🤯

Six impossible things about chess

1Start with the mathematics, because it is the most frightening part. After each player has made just one move, 400 different positions are possible. After two moves each, there are 72,084. After three, over nine million. After four, more than 288 billion. The total number of games that could theoretically be played is somewhere around 10120 — a number so enormous that it comfortably beats the number of atoms in the observable universe. Every time you sit down to play, you are almost certainly making a game that has never been played before in human history.

2Real games, fortunately, are shorter — though not always. The longest tournament game on record was played in Belgrade in 1989 between Ivan Nikolić and Goran Arsović. It lasted 269 moves and more than twenty hours, and after all that effort it ended in a draw. In theory, a single game could last 5,949 moves before the rules force it to stop.

3Chess also travels well. In June 1970 the cosmonauts of Soyuz-9 played a game against two colleagues on the ground — the first chess match between space and Earth. It, too, was a draw. Back on this planet, the German-born player Timur Gareyev once played 48 games at the same time blindfolded, riding an exercise bike while he did it.

4Machines took longer to arrive. In 1997 an IBM computer called Deep Blue beat the World Champion Garry Kasparov 3½–2½. Kasparov, who had won easily the year before, could not believe that a machine had played so creatively and accused IBM of cheating; no evidence was ever found. Today a free chess app on an ordinary phone is far stronger than Deep Blue ever was, and no human being has any realistic chance against it.

5Finally, chess is bigger than ever. About 600 million adults play, and when the Netflix series The Queen's Gambit came out in 2020, sales of chess sets rose by more than 100% and millions of new players appeared online. For a game invented for kings and fought over by bishops, that is not a bad result after fifteen centuries.

14 Language focus · The language of history

How to talk about the past of things 🏛️

Every text you have just read is built on three grammar patterns. Olympiad papers love all three.

1 · Passive voice

We use it when who did it is unknown or unimportant — exactly what happens in history.

  • Chess was invented in India.
  • The game is played by 600 million people.
  • The rules have been changed many times.
  • The queen was given new powers.
be + V3by + agent

2 · Reporting beliefs

When we are not sure, English has ready-made phrases:

  • It is said that a wise man invented it.
  • The game is believed to have come from India.
  • He is said to have invented the folding board.
  • Apparently / Allegedly, Napoleon lost to a machine.

3 · Dates & periods

  • in 1886 · in the 15th century · in the 1970s
  • by the 10th century (к…)
  • It dates back to the 6th century.
  • The game is 1,500 years old / it goes back 1,500 years.
  • a 15th-century book (no -s in the adjective!)
